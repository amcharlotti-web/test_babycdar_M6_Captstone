You asked us two things: where to put commercial resource across the APAC pet-food portfolio, and how much premium volume to plan to supply through 2028.

Our answer, up front, is this. Growth in this market is concentrated in a specific group of categories we call the Growth Engines — that’s where the return on investment is. And premium demand is not plateauing: we should plan for roughly 359 million kilograms of premium volume by 2028, and build in headroom above that.

The rest of this is five minutes on how we know, and what we’d ask you to decide. Markets covered: Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, Thailand.

The first thing we learned is that thinking about this market as simply “premium versus economy” hides the real structure.

When we group the twelve categories by how fast they’ve grown and how big they are, four distinct segments fall out — and this grouping is statistically clean, not eyeballed.

[Point at chart.] The one that matters commercially is the Growth Engines — five dry-food categories that combine the strongest sustained growth with meaningful scale. That’s the rare combination: things that are growing fast are usually small, and things that are big are usually flat. This segment is both.

Contrast that with our single largest category, Economy Dry Dog Food — it’s a huge volume business, but essentially flat. It’s an anchor to defend, not a place to chase growth. And one category, Economy Wet Dog Food, is actually shrinking.

The headline: allocate by segment, not by price tier.

Finding 2 — Premium demand keeps rising

  • Premium’s share of APAC volume rose and held — a level shift, not a blip.

Second finding: this isn’t a passing trend.

Premium’s share of total volume rose over the last decade and then held at that higher level rather than falling back. That pattern — a step up that doesn’t revert — is what we’d expect from a durable change in consumer behaviour, not a temporary spike.

And if you want a picture of where this goes, look at Singapore. It’s already around 62% premium, versus about 31% across the region. No economy tier at all. Singapore isn’t an outlier to explain away — it’s a preview of the direction of travel, and a template for how a premium-led market behaves.

Our forecast points to steady growth of roughly 7.7 million kilograms a year, reaching about 359 million kilograms of premium volume by 2028.

What this means — where the money goes

  1. Invest behind the Growth Engines — best growth and scale.
  2. Fund capacity to ~359 M kg premium by 2028, contingency to ~364.
  3. Selectively grow premium & mid-priced wet cat food — small but rising.
  4. Defend Economy Dry Dog Food — protect the volume anchor, don’t over-invest.
  5. Rationalise Economy Wet Dog Food — the only category in decline.

So here’s where we’d point the resource.

First and most important: invest behind the Growth Engines. That’s the one place that offers both growth and scale, so it’s where commercial spend earns the most.

Second: fund premium capacity toward 359 million kilograms by 2028, with headroom to 364.

Third: a smaller, targeted bet — premium and mid-priced wet cat food. Growing nicely, still small, so it’s a selective play, not a big commitment.

Fourth: defend, don’t over-feed, our big Economy Dry Dog anchor. Protect share and run it efficiently, but don’t pour growth money into a flat market.

And fifth: take a hard look at Economy Wet Dog Food. It’s the only thing shrinking.

The theme running through all five: match the investment to the segment.

How confident are we?

  • The forecast is a trend continuation — reliable if premiumisation holds.
  • We stress-tested it with economic and demographic data (income, urbanisation, birth rates). It did not improve the forecast — so we’re not over-engineering.
  • Plan to the upper band, not the point estimate. That’s the honest number.

Key assumption: the premium shift continues. If consumer spending turns sharply, we revisit.

Let me be candid about confidence, because a forecast you can’t question isn’t worth much.

Our model is essentially a disciplined continuation of the last decade’s trend. That’s a sound, robust approach on the data we have — but it does assume the premiumisation shift keeps going. If consumer spending turned sharply, that assumption would be the first thing to break.

We also tested whether adding economic and demographic drivers — income, urbanisation, birth rates — would sharpen the forecast. They didn’t; if anything they widened the uncertainty. That’s a useful negative result: it tells us we’re not missing an obvious driver, and it keeps us from over-engineering a model on limited history.

The practical upshot is the one I gave earlier: plan to the top of the range, not the midpoint. The cost of being slightly over on capacity is far lower than the cost of being caught short in a growing premium market.
